First article inspection, explained.

A first article inspection is how you prove, on the first part you make, that your process can build what the drawing says. Customers ask for it by name, and the paperwork is where small shops get written up.

What It Is

Make one part the way you'll make them all, then measure everything.

Before you ship a run of a new part, you make the first one the way you'll make all of them. Then you measure every single thing the drawing calls out and write it down. That's a first article inspection, or FAI. The format most aerospace customers expect comes from a standard called AS9102, and it has three forms.

It isn't a check on how good your people are. It's a check on whether your drawing, your material, your process and your measurements all agree, and whether you can show it.

When You Need One

A new part number is the obvious reason. These others catch shops off guard.

The customer changes the drawing or the revision, and the change affects fit, form or function.
You change how the part gets made: the process, where it gets made, what it's made on, the tooling, the machine program, or who makes it, including a supplier you send work to.
Production stops for a long stretch. The standard calls it two years, and your customer can set a different number.
Your customer's purchase order adds its own triggers.

AS9102 lists the triggers, and your customer can add to them. Check the current revision of the standard and the PO before you assume you're covered.

The Three Forms

What each form carries.

Form 1: the part

Which part number, which revision, which drawing, and which assemblies it goes into. It proves everyone was talking about the same part. It's also the form that gets signed last. Signing Form 1 locks the other two.

Form 2: what went into it

The material, the special processes like welding, heat treat, NDT or plating, the functional tests, and who did each one. If a supplier did a process for you, their approval and certificate go here.

Form 3: every characteristic

Every dimension and note on the drawing gets a number on a ballooned copy of the print, then a line on the form with what you measured and whether it passed. Expect most of your time to go here.

Where It Goes Wrong

Six places shops get written up.

These are the spots on a first article inspection where I'd expect a small shop to get written up.

A characteristic that never got a balloon

Something on the drawing, a note, a tolerance block, a finish call, has no balloon, so nothing on Form 3 matches it. It's the easiest one for an auditor to find: they take the print and count.

Measured to an old revision

The measurements were done to a drawing revision that wasn't current that day. The part may be fine. The record says you didn't check it against the right print.

A special process with no approval record

A process shows up on Form 2 with no record of who was approved to do it. Welding, heat treat, NDT and plating all need the same thing: who, when, and the evidence they were qualified.

A supplier's work with no evidence

A supplier made part of it, and there's no certificate or report for their part. Their work is your work in the prime's eyes.

Nobody owns the form

The form gets filled out by whoever happens to be free. Forms filled out by different people look like it: different habits, different gaps, and nobody who can explain the last one.

Something changed and nobody completed a new first article

A process, a machine, a supplier or a drawing revision changed, and the old first article is still the one on file. The triggers above are the list to check against.

A Quick Check

If you can say yes to these, you're in better shape than you think.

We know which jobs need a first article and which customers ask for one.
Someone owns the form, and it's the same person or role every time.
Every characteristic on the drawing is numbered on a ballooned print and measured against it.
We redo the first article when something significant changes.
The drawing revision we measured to is the one that was current that day.

If you're not sure, take the AS9100 Gap Check. It has a whole section on first article, and it takes about four minutes. Or call me and we'll go through your last one together.
