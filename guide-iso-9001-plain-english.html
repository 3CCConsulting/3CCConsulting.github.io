ISO 9001 in plain English

What your customer is actually asking for when they say the words — and what the standard does and does not require of a twelve-person shop.

Start Here

ISO 9001 is not about quality. It is about consistency.

This trips people up. The standard does not tell you how good your welds have to be, what tolerance to hold, or which materials to use. You decide that. Your customer decides that.

What the standard asks is narrower and, honestly, harder: can you do it the same way every time, prove that you did, and catch it when you didn't?

A shop can make excellent parts and fail an ISO audit. A shop can make mediocre parts and pass one. That sounds backwards until you realize what the customer is buying — not your best day, but the promise that every day looks like it.

The Three Questions

Everything in the standard comes back to these

1. Do you say what you do?

Is the way work actually gets done written down somewhere a new hire could follow — or does it live in one person's head?

2. Do you do what you say?

Does the crew actually follow it, or is the written version a fiction that comes out for audits?

3. Can you prove it?

When someone asks what happened on a job eighteen months ago, can you show them — with records, not memory?

The Structure

What the seven sections actually mean

ISO 9001 is organized into ten clauses. The first three are definitions and scope. The real requirements are in clauses 4 through 10. Here they are without the language.

4 — Context. Who are your customers, what do they require of you, who else has a say (regulators, insurers, primes), and what part of your business does this cover?
5 — Leadership. Does the owner actually back this, is someone named as responsible, and does everyone know who decides what?
6 — Planning. What could go wrong, what are you doing about it, and what are you trying to improve this year?
7 — Support. Do you have the people, training, equipment, and calibration to do the work — and do you control your documents so the crew is not building from a superseded drawing?
8 — Operation. The actual work. How jobs get reviewed before you accept them, how you control your suppliers and subs, how you inspect, and what happens to nonconforming work.
9 — Performance. What do you measure, do you audit yourself, and does management sit down and look at it on a schedule?
10 — Improvement. When something goes wrong, do you fix the cause or just the part?
Common Misreadings

Four things people believe that are not true

"It requires mountains of paperwork." The standard requires documented information where it is needed to control the work. Small organizations are explicitly allowed to keep it proportionate. The mountains come from consultants selling templates, not from ISO.
"We're too small." There is no size threshold anywhere in the standard. A twelve-person shop can be certified. The system just has to fit the twelve people.
"We need software." No. Records can be paper, a spreadsheet, or whatever you already own. Most small shops already pay for tools that would do it and never set them up.
"Certified means good." It means consistent and documented. Your reputation still does the rest of the work.
The Question To Ask

Certified, or compliant?

When a customer says "we need you ISO 9001," they mean one of two very different things:

Actually certified

An accredited registrar audits you and issues a certificate. Real cost, real timeline, annual surveillance audits after that. Some contracts genuinely require it.

Compliant, not certified

You build and run a system that meets the requirements, and you show it to the customer directly. No registrar, no certificate, far less cost. This satisfies a surprising number of the people asking.

Call and ask which one they mean before you spend anything. The difference is thousands of dollars and several months, and the person who sent the packet usually knows.
