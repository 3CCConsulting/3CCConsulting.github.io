AS9100 is becoming IA9100

The aerospace quality standard is being renamed and revised at the same time, following the ISO 9001 update it is built on. Here is what is changing, what it means for a small supplier, and what is worth doing now.

Status

Where this actually stands

The IAQG is revising the aerospace quality series and renaming it at the same time. AS9100 becomes IA9100 — "IA" for International Aerospace. The whole family moves with it: AS9110 (maintenance, repair, overhaul) becomes IA9110, AS9120 (distributors) becomes IA9120, and AS9145 (APQP/PPAP) becomes IA9145.

The rename does more than change a number. AS9100, EN9100, and JISQ9100 were the Americas, Europe, and Asia designations for what was already meant to be one standard. IA9100 collapses them into a single name.

Timing, stated honestly: published sources do not agree. IA9100 is tied to ISO 9001:2026 and follows it. Estimates for the aerospace standard currently run from a final draft in late 2026 to a release around mid-2027. Nobody selling you a date right now is reading a published document.

One thing that is not in dispute: the IAQG has signaled that IA9100 will move to frequent, smaller updates rather than one large revision every decade. That is the change with the longest tail, and almost nobody is planning for it. More on that below.

The Changes

Six themes worth knowing about

IA9100 inherits everything changing in ISO 9001:2026 — risks and opportunities separated, quality culture and ethical behavior made explicit, strategic direction emphasized, climate folded into organizational context, Annex A rewritten. That is covered here. On top of that, the aerospace layer adds the following.

Open any one to see what it means for a small supplier. The order is roughly how much work each one is likely to be for a shop that is already certified.

Cybersecurity becomes a quality requirement

Information security moves into the standard itself — risk assessment, secure access control, incident response. This is the single biggest genuinely new addition.

Why it lands hardest: it is the one most small shops have nothing written for. If your entire answer today is "we have antivirus," that is the gap. It is also the only theme here that asks you to document something you may not currently do at all, rather than write down something you already do.

Supplier oversight extends past your direct suppliers

Sub-tier visibility, traceability, and counterfeit-part controls all tighten. You become responsible for knowing where material came from before it reached the supplier you actually buy from.

Remote and digital auditing of suppliers is expected to be explicitly allowed, which cuts both ways — it is cheaper for you, and it makes your customer's audit of you easier to schedule.

Statistical methods get real teeth

Process capability studies, statistical process control, measurement system analysis, and design of experiments are all emphasized more heavily.

The practical effect: "we inspect every one" stops being an adequate answer to a capability question. Sorting good parts from bad is not the same as demonstrating a process that makes good parts, and the revision draws that line harder.

Product safety gets promoted

Safety-critical item management and hazard reporting — including anonymous reporting routes — move toward the center of the standard rather than sitting in a clause off to the side.

If you make anything flight-critical, expect the auditor to ask how somebody on your floor raises a concern without putting their name on it.

Quality culture and human factors get formalized

Leadership commitment, human factors analysis, and ethical conduct become things an auditor asks about directly, not things inferred from your records.

Most shops with a decent culture already pass this in substance and fail it on evidence. The work is showing it, not building it.

Sustainability enters the frame

Environmental management and life-cycle considerations are expected to be integrated rather than kept in a separate ISO 14001 world.

For most small suppliers this is a documentation exercise, not an operational one — but it has to be addressed rather than ignored. Listed last here because it is usually the least work.

The Part Nobody Is Talking About

Build for the next revision, not just this one.

If IA9100 really does move to frequent, smaller updates, then a quality system with clause numbers written into the body of every procedure becomes a maintenance problem that never ends. Every small revision turns into a document-control project.

There is a straightforward way to avoid that, and it costs nothing to adopt now: keep clause references in one mapping table, and out of your procedure text. Your SOPs describe how the work is done. A single crosswalk document says which clause each one satisfies. When the standard shifts, you update one table instead of forty documents.

This is how I build every system, for exactly this reason. It is worth doing whether or not you ever hire anybody.

If You Are Already Certified

Two calls to make, and one thing to watch.

Revisions of this kind come with a transition window during which existing certificates stay valid and you move over at a surveillance or recertification audit. The IAQG has not announced the IA9100 window yet. ISO 9001 is expected to run three years; the aerospace window is widely expected to be shorter, precisely because of the move to more frequent updates.

Call your registrar and ask two questions: what is my transition deadline, and which of my scheduled audits will be the transition audit? Both answers should be free.

The thing to watch: AS9104/1 — the standard governing the certification bodies themselves — is being updated as IA9104/1, and it is expected to have auditors verifying things like on-time delivery, customer satisfaction, and quality metrics at audit. That is a shift in what your auditor arrives asking for. If your performance data lives in your head or in a spreadsheet somebody rebuilds each quarter, that is worth fixing before it is a finding.

If You Are Not Certified Yet

This is better timing than it looks.

If a prime or a Tier 1 has just started asking and you are building from scratch, you are in better shape than a shop that certified in 2018 and now has to change. You get to build against current thinking once instead of building twice.

Build on the parts that are not changing — documented process, records and traceability, nonconformance and corrective action, configuration management, first article inspection, special process control, internal audit, management review. That is most of the work, and none of it is in play. Treat the new themes as additions to a foundation, not as a different foundation.

And be clear-eyed about which half usually fails. AS9100 contains all of ISO 9001 plus roughly a hundred aerospace-specific requirements. Small suppliers rarely fail the ISO half. They fail on configuration control, counterfeit prevention, first article inspection, and special process qualification — the additions nobody warned them about.

What To Do Now

Three things, none of which cost money

1

Call your registrar

If you are certified: get your transition deadline and your transition audit date in writing, and ask what IA9104/1 will change about how they audit you.

2

Start on information security

Of everything coming, this is the addition most small suppliers have nothing written for. Documenting who has access to what, and what you do when something goes wrong, costs nothing and will be required.

3

Wait on the rest

Do not buy an IA9100 transition package before the standard publishes. Anyone selling a definitive gap analysis against an unpublished document is selling a guess.
