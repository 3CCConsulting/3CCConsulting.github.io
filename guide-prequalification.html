What a prequalification packet actually asks for

A general contractor or plant owner sends you a packet and says fill this out to stay on the bid list. Here is what is usually inside, why they want it, and what to do about the parts you cannot answer.

First, The Honest Part

Most of it you already have. Some of it you don't.

Packets vary by owner, but they cluster into six groups. The first three are paperwork you can pull from a drawer or ask your agent for. The last three are where small contractors get stuck — and they are the reason a qualified shop loses a job it could have done.

Group 1 — Easy

Company identity

Legal business name, entity type, state of formation, years in business
Federal tax ID and a signed W-9
Trade licenses and registrations, with numbers and expiration dates
Ownership and any diversity classifications, if applicable

Why they ask: they need to know who they are actually contracting with, and that you are allowed to do the work in that jurisdiction.

Group 2 — Your Agent Has These

Insurance and bonding

Certificate of insurance — general liability, auto, workers' compensation, and often umbrella or excess
Specific coverage limits, which frequently exceed what you carry today
Additional-insured and waiver-of-subrogation language
Bonding capacity — single job and aggregate — usually as a letter from your surety

Why they ask: if something goes wrong on their site, they want to know it lands on your policy and not theirs. Where people get stuck: the required limits are higher than what you carry. That is a phone call to your agent, not a dead end.

Group 3 — Your Accountant Has These

Financial capacity

Financial statements, often two or three years, sometimes reviewed or audited
Bank reference and line of credit
Largest project completed, and largest currently under contract
Current backlog

Why they ask: they are checking whether a job this size would break you. A shop that runs out of money mid-project is their problem, not just yours.

Group 4 — Where It Starts To Hurt

Safety record and safety program

EMR — experience modification rate — for the last three years, on carrier letterhead
OSHA 300 and 300A logs, usually three years
Recordable incident rate and lost-time rate
Any OSHA citations, with what you did about them
A written safety program or manual
Training records — competent person, confined space, fall protection, lockout/tagout, whatever the work requires
Drug and alcohol testing policy

Why they ask: your EMR is a number that follows you, and a bad one can disqualify you before anyone reads the rest. Where people get stuck: the numbers exist, but there is no written program behind them, and no organized training file.

Group 5 — The One Nobody Is Ready For

Quality program

A written quality manual or quality management system description
ISO 9001 certificate, or a statement of when you intend to have one
How you handle nonconforming work — who catches it, who dispositions it, where it is recorded
Corrective action process, and evidence you have actually used it
Inspection and test procedures, and calibration records for anything that measures
Welder or operator qualification records, current and traceable
Document and revision control — how you know the crew is working from the current drawing
Named quality representative

Why they ask: they are being audited by their customer, and your work becomes part of their evidence. If you cannot show a system, you become their risk.

Where people get stuck: everywhere. The work is good and the knowledge is real, but nothing is written down. This is the group that most often turns a qualified shop into a declined one.

Group 6 — Judgment Calls

Experience and references

Project history — scope, dollar value, dates, owner contact
References they will actually call
Key personnel with resumes and certifications
Subcontractors you would use, and how you qualify them
Any litigation, terminations, or liens
What To Do

If you can't answer Group 5

Three things, in this order.

First

Answer the packet honestly

Do not leave a section blank and do not overstate. "In development, target date [X]" is a real answer and buyers see it regularly. A blank looks like you did not read it. A false claim ends the relationship when they audit you.

Second

Ask what they actually need

Call the person who sent it. Sometimes "ISO 9001 certified" means certified. Sometimes it means "show us a documented system." Those are very different amounts of work, and nobody tells you which unless you ask.

Third

Fix it before the next one

There is always a next packet. The shop that builds the system once answers every future packet in an afternoon instead of a weekend.
